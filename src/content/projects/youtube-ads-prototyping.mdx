Testing a new YouTube ad format starts with a working prototype — something a research participant can actually click, scroll, and react to. The Ads UX Engineering team had built an internal prototyping tool for exactly this. It worked, but only if you already thought like an engineer: it assumed working knowledge of HTML and CSS, so in practice only our own team used it, and every prototype still cost engineering hours.

I worked on this tool from both sides. As a UX engineer on the team, I built and shipped production features on the live tool — in Lit and TypeScript — and used it to deliver prototypes for real research studies. In parallel, I designed its next generation: the version UX designers and researchers could pick up without us. I left Google before the redesign shipped, so this case study covers both the production work that shipped and the design work I handed off.

## Discovery & research

Learning the tool as a newcomer, I hit the walls firsthand: no obvious starting point, an assumed vocabulary of HTML and CSS, and "next steps" that were anything but obvious. The tool expected you to already know what everything meant.

<Figure src="/projects/prototyping-add-variables-current-flow.png" alt="An annotated walkthrough of the flow for adding variables in the old tool" caption="Tracking the current flow for adding new variables, step by step, and noting where a first-time user would stall." />

Then I talked to the people the tool was supposed to serve. UX designers and researchers weren't just untrained on it — they were unconvinced. They had UX engineers to build prototypes for them; learning a new tool was a cost with no obvious payoff.

That reframed the bar for the redesign: it couldn't just be *possible* for non-engineers to use — it had to be *easier than asking us*. I also ran a competitive analysis of Framer and ProtoPie, and the shared pattern was striking: both aggressively reduce cognitive load by revealing complexity progressively, never all at once.

## Design principles

The redesign came down to a few commitments:

- Start from YouTube components, not empty boxes — nobody in Ads should begin from scratch
- Reveal complexity progressively; hide advanced settings until they're asked for
- Group functionality so screens read as a handful of decisions, not a wall of settings
- Borrow Figma's interaction grammar — designers should feel like they already know the tool
- Make the interface legible to newcomers: clear imagery, icons, and tooltips over assumed knowledge

## Where it landed

I left Google in October 2025, before the redesign shipped. The production features I built along the way are part of the live tool.

What the work established:

- **A sharper problem definition.** The adoption barrier wasn't missing capability — it was prerequisite knowledge. Every design decision followed from removing that prerequisite.
- **A tested design direction.** Components-first authoring, progressive disclosure, and a Figma-native interaction grammar, validated against the exact moments where real users stalled.
- **The case for the audience.** The research documented *why* designers and researchers weren't converting, giving the team a target for the next version rather than a hunch.
