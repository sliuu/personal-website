## The problem

Waymo's ride-hailing service was live in San Francisco and Phoenix, and in-app feedback kept pointing at the same sore spot: pickups and dropoffs. Riders were telling us *that* these moments went wrong — a bad corner, a confusing walk, a car that stopped somewhere unexpected — but not *why*, and not what separated a good pickup from a bad one.

The UX Research team was asked to find out.

<Challenge text="Understand how pickups and dropoffs correlate with rider satisfaction, identify what makes one good or bad, and find directions for improvement." />

## The study

We ran a diary study: for four weeks, 100 riders completed a survey about the pickup and dropoff of every Waymo ride they took. Participants were split evenly between San Francisco and Phoenix and between new and existing riders, with deliberate coverage across age, race/ethnicity, and disability.

Four weeks × every ride × 100 people adds up — the design gave us real quantitative volume, not a handful of anecdotes.

<Figure src="/projects/pickup-dropoff-survey.png" alt="Sample survey questions asked to riders after each ride" caption="A sample of the questions riders answered after every trip." />

## My role

I joined at 20% alongside my engineering role, working with Waymo's Head of Customer Research — and I was the only quantitative researcher on the study:

<div class="cols-3">
  <Callout icon="users" text="Conducted initial hour-long user interviews and onboarded participants into the diary study format" />
  <Callout icon="chart" text="Wrote the scripts that compiled survey data and cross-referenced every response with trip logs" />
  <Callout icon="easel" text="Analyzed the data, drafted the reports, and presented findings to cross-functional teams" />
</div>

The cross-referencing was the quietly load-bearing part: joining self-reported survey answers to logged trip data (in Python and SQL) meant every subjective rating came anchored to what the car actually did.

## Key insights

<div class="cols-3">
  <Callout icon="car" text="Pickups and dropoffs caused real rider anxiety — blocking traffic, uncertain locations, awkward waits" />
  <Callout icon="arrowsSplit" text="Last-minute location changes hurt satisfaction faster than raw distance from the destination" />
  <Callout icon="trendDown" text="Riders had learned to expect more timing uncertainty from Waymo than from other ride-hailing apps" />
</div>

That middle finding was the headline: riders could forgive a longer walk they knew about, but a spot that *changed on them* broke trust. Satisfaction tracked certainty, not just distance.

## Impact

After the readout, we assembled designers, engineers, researchers, and PMs to turn findings into direction. The clearest takeaways: onboard and offboard systems needed tighter coordination, and the app needed to communicate more honestly about uncertainty — because expectation-setting, not just spot-picking, drove satisfaction.
