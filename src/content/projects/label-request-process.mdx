## The problem

Human-labeled data powers self-driving: it's how what the car sees becomes something a model can learn from. Labels range from 2D bounding boxes to timestamps to open questions about a run — and every new label type starts with an ML engineer explaining to a group of labelers exactly what they need and how to do it.

Historically, the labeling team hand-held every ML engineer through most of their labeling requests. That worked when the company was small. As Waymo grew, it became a bottleneck with a queue: obtuse documentation, tribal knowledge, and a labeling engineer required every time someone needed something new.

## Key insights

Charting the current process revealed the first problem before any interview did: there were almost as many entry points into "make a labeling request" as there were users.

<Figure src="/projects/label-request-process-flow.png" alt="Flow chart of the existing labeling request process, showing many divergent entry points" />

From there I inventoried every piece of information a request actually needs, grouped the related settings, and separated what users change constantly from the advanced settings they touch once a year — the raw material for a form that doesn't overwhelm.

The interviews surfaced a quieter problem: vocabulary. Users didn't understand the terms on screens like the one below, and the documentation that would have explained them was buried in tooltips.

<Figure src="/projects/labeling-requesting-old.png" alt="The old label requesting page: a dense form full of unexplained technical terms" />

## The redesign

I designed the request flow as a wizard: related settings grouped into steps, documentation promoted from tooltips into the interface itself, and advanced settings tucked behind a deliberate door.

## What the work established

I handed the design off before leaving Waymo. {/* CONFIRM: replace with the shipped story if the wizard went live before you left. */}

- **One path in.** The design collapsed the tangle of entry points into a single hub where requests are created and tracked — the flow chart's answer.
- **Documentation as interface.** Explanations moved out of tooltips and into the moments where users make decisions, so the form teaches the vocabulary it uses.
- **Overwhelm as a design budget.** The common/advanced split came straight from research data on what users actually touch, not from guesses about what looks simpler.

The intended effect: ML engineers who can make and track their own requests, and labeling engineers who get their time back. {/* CONFIRM: if you know this happened after launch, we can state it as a result instead of an intention. */}
